ORIGIN & COERCION ARCHITECTURE

A brilliant Ukrainian nuclear physicist and chemist abducted by the KGB to spearhead the covert Soviet antimatter weapons programme beneath Chernobyl Reactor 4. His wife Kateryna and eight-year-old daughter Nina were detained under Officer Sokolov's 1983 order to guarantee compliance. Surviving the April 26 explosion at ground zero, Yadro hunts for his family and the officials who signed the detention papers while battling accelerating memory blackouts.

⚛ ELEMENTAL MANIPULATION
Can alter and transmute atomic structures on the periodic table, strictly limited to elements whose chemical composition and crystal lattice he understands.
☢ IONISING RADIATION
Emits lethal bursts of gamma and neutron radiation at will, destroying living organic tissue and melting electronics in his immediate proximity.
🔊 SENSORY MOTIF: GEIGER SIGNATURE
Whenever radiation is emitted, an unmistakable Geiger counter clicking sounds as a terrifying auditory motif and final warning before death.
🧠 COGNITIVE DECAY (THE COST)
Continuous power usage triggers mental deterioration, amnesia, and blackouts. His mind and faculties only regenerate when he ceases power usage.
THE INTENTIONAL "GAP" // FORENSIC NEGATIVE CAPABILITY (LONG, 2007)

Two foundational questions are deliberately withheld from canon across all media: How could a mortal body survive the physical epicenter of a graphite reactor explosion? and How long can his cells survive before radiation self-destruction consumes him?

Anatoly Yadro | Private notebook

3 May 1986

I paid a man twelve roubles for this notebook and a shirt. He wanted another five for the shirt because I had got blood on it while begging him to sell me the damn thing. Fair enough.

I have been sleeping in an empty house outside the town. I went back to our flat yesterday but the building was deserted. Nina's shoes were still by the door. I took her school photograph and a clean pair of trousers and I honestly don't know why I took the photograph when I already have one in my wallet, but she looks older in this one or maybe I was hoping that if I had enough reminders of her with me she would just appear in front of me.

There is something wrong with my hands. This morning I dropped a spoon into a cup of tea and when I picked it out the handle was almost flat. I remember being annoyed that it had fallen in, and I remember holding it, but I cannot account for the force necessary to flatten it. There was no pain and either way I have put it aside for now and examine it later, right now I need to come up with a plan to find my girls and surely they are looking for me. I don't know who else is looking for me too but I'm not going to stop until I find my girls.

19 May 1986

The spoon was aluminium, I found another in the kitchen and managed to bend it without exerting any pressure. It took most of the afternoon to do the same with an iron nail, although I suspect it was the alloy that gave me trouble. The key to the back door refuses to cooperate. I have no idea what the damned thing is made of.

I went back to the plant today and managed to sneak inside for a dosimeter and I'm really hoping no one saw me. It behaved normally until I held the nail but these cheap things that the state reluctantly gave to the plants only go up to 3.6 roentgens and if what I think is happening to me is actually happening then this thing is about as useful as poultice to a dead man. I repeated the experiment at the other end of the room with much the same result and I wanted to try something else but I felt too sick and went to bed. I'm not sure if it had to do with what I'm trying now or the blast from a month ago and I still don't know what that bright light I saw from the anti-matter container was right after the reactor explosion but either way I must be more careful with this.

A man at the district office claims there is no record of Kateryna or Nina among the evacuees. I asked him to check the records from 1983 and he looked at me as though I had asked him for the contents of his mother's purse. I left before I lost my temper.

7 September 1986

I have found the name of the officer who authorised the detention order. Sokolov's signature is on the second page, where he has misspelt Kateryna's patronymic. Three years and he never bothered to learn her name properly.

I had better keep the papers somewhere separate from this book. Yesterday I woke up on the kitchen floor with the cupboard doors lying beside me. I had written the date on my wrist before going to sleep, which was fortunate because I was convinced it was still Thursday. It was Saturday. I have no recollection of Friday whatsoever. I feel like I'm running out of time, they could be anywhere by now and I really hope I make it in time for Nina's birthday, I promised her a pair of roller skates but that was so long ago now maybe Katya already bought her some.

16 January 1987

Nina's birthday was last week. I bought a small box of coloured pencils and felt rather foolish afterwards. She will be twelve now, I have no idea whether she still draws or what she even looks like.

I went to see a former clerk from the directorate. He knew the file number and admitted that there were other families like mine, although he insisted that he had never seen the people whose papers passed through his office. I asked him what he thought happened to them. He started crying. I hadn't even touched him.

The meter was making that noise again. I could hear it through my coat, so he must have heard it too. I left it on his desk. I hope he is all right. I don't know why I wrote that.

2 August 1988

I am getting better at it. The difficulty with unfamiliar materials remains, but I can now do things with ordinary steel without having to sit down afterwards, provided I stop in time. The problem is that I don't always know when I have stopped. I found a length of wire wound round my left arm yesterday and it took me several minutes to realise that I had put it there myself.

Someone has been moving the papers. The arrest order was underneath the photograph this morning. That is where I usually put it, which is precisely why I moved it last night. I remember moving it.

I have made a list of the men who signed the orders. There are seven names so far and I have left a space for the director's superior. I want them out of their offices. I want their wives to know what they have been doing. I want Sokolov to have to ask somebody else for permission to see his children.

11 October 1989

I have been trying to establish what happened on Monday. The newspaper mentions an incident at a military depot and I have a piece of paper in my pocket with the name of the town on it. I cannot remember going there. There is mud on my boots which proves absolutely nothing.

I dreamt that Nina was behind the wall again. She was complaining about the cold and I kept telling her to move back so I could get her out. When I woke up there was plaster in the bed and my fingers were bleeding. I searched the room next door even though I knew perfectly well that it was empty.

Why did I go to that town? I must have had a reason. I wouldn't have gone without a reason.

28 October 1989

There were three soldiers sitting at the table behind me in the café this afternoon. I wasn't paying much attention until one of them mentioned the depot. Apparently, several men had been admitted to hospital with radiation burns and the entire premises had been sealed off. One of the soldiers claimed that his brother had been stationed there and that the steel doors to the records room had been found twisted beyond recognition. The others told him to keep his voice down.

I asked him whether he knew what had been kept in that room. He looked at me rather strangely and said he didn't know. I must have sounded desperate because he moved his chair away from me.

I have been thinking about those doors all evening. If I went there looking for records of Katya and Nina, surely I would have brought something back? I have emptied my pockets and searched through every piece of paper in this room at least four times. There is nothing.

Perhaps I found something I did not want to know. I hope that isn't the case. I would rather believe I killed those men for nothing than believe I found what I was looking for and have forgotten it.

23 February 1991

Found Kovalenko's address. I remember him from the ventilation crew, though in the photograph he looks much older than I remember and for a moment I thought it was his father. I have made copies of the orders. He can get them to the radio stations. I cannot go myself because people become ill around me and I am beginning to suspect that I do not notice when it starts.

There was a woman on the tram yesterday who complained of a metallic taste in her mouth. I got off at the next stop and walked home. I wonder how many people I have made ill without knowing it.

I keep forgetting what I have already posted. There are envelopes all over the table and I opened one this morning expecting a reply from Kateryna. It was addressed to Kovalenko in my own handwriting. I sat there for nearly an hour trying to remember why she would have sent him a letter.

I ought to write the addresses here but anybody could find this. Somebody has found it already. There is a thumbprint on the photograph.

14 March 1991

The broadcast was on last night. I heard the name Yadro and turned the volume up and by the time I had fetched the notebook they were talking about something else. I don't know whether Kovalenko sent the papers or whether the police have got him. I must not go to his house. I must remember that.

There is a photograph of Sokolov in one of the newspapers. He has put on weight. Imagine being able to eat properly for eight years with that on your conscience. He is standing beside a man whose name I have written down somewhere, and I have been through every page of this book looking for it, but the more I look the more certain I am that I have seen the man in our flat. Kateryna would remember him. I need to ask her.

Someone knocked on the door this morning and called me by my name. It occurred to me afterwards that it might have been Kovalenko, although I cannot remember ever giving him this address.

I can hear the clicking even without the meter now. Last night I put it in the cupboard and shut the door and it continued, so I took the batteries out. I could still hear it. I cannot concentrate long enough to read the numbers on the orders. Four-B. I know that one. I knew it yesterday.

15 March 1991 (I think)

I have written Nina's age on the wall and changed it twice. She was eight when they took her. I keep buying things for an eight-year-old child and I can't remember what a girl of sixteen likes. I was her father. I ought to know.

Would I even recognise her in the street? She might have cut her hair. Perhaps she looks more like Katya now. I tried to remember her voice this morning but could only remember her crying. I cannot remember what she was crying about. Was I there when they took her?

They have started talking about reforms again on the radio as though a different man in an office makes any difference to the families still waiting outside those buildings. I want the whole filthy business dragged out where people can see it, every name and every signature, and if those men think they can retire and disappear into their nice little flats they are mistaken. I will find Sokolov. I will find him and he will tell me where she is, where both of them are, and I must remember to ask before I do anything to him.

Before. Write it down. Ask him first.

Kateryna. Nina. Where are you my loves?
